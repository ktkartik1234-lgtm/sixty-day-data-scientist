A real model's loss depends on every parameter at once — $J(\theta_1, \dots,
\theta_d)$ with $d$ in the thousands or billions. Nobody can picture that
surface, and nobody needs to: calculus handles it by staying disciplined and
changing one thing at a time.

## Functions of several variables

A multivariable function eats a vector and returns one number. The squared
error of a linear model is the canonical example:

$$
J(w, b) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(w x_i + b - y_i\big)^2
$$

Plot it and you get a surface. Mark every point where $J = c$ for a few
constants $c$ and you get **level sets** — the contour lines of a topographic
map. Keep that picture; it pays off in a moment.

## Hold everything else fixed

The partial derivative of $f$ with respect to $x$ is the ordinary derivative,
taken while every other variable is frozen in place:

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = \lim_{h \to 0}
\frac{f(x+h,\; y) - f(x,\; y)}{h}
$$

All the single-variable rules apply unchanged — you just treat the other
symbols as constants. For $f(x, y) = 3x^2 y$:

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 6xy \qquad\quad
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 3x^2
$$

## The gradient — and the Jacobian

Stack the $d$ partials into a vector, the **gradient**:

$$
\nabla f = \begin{bmatrix}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1} \\
\vdots \\
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_d}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Two facts make it the most reused object in machine learning:

- **Direction.** $\nabla f$ points in the direction of steepest *increase*
  and is perpendicular to the level sets. So $-\nabla f$ points downhill,
  straight across the contours of that topographic map.
- **Magnitude.** Its length $\lVert \nabla f \rVert$ is the slope in that
  direction — how steep "steepest" actually is at this point.

When the function returns a vector instead of a scalar, the collection of
all partials becomes a matrix — the **Jacobian**, with entries
$J_{ij} = \partial f_i \, / \, \partial x_j$. Every layer of a neural network
is vector-valued, so backpropagation is largely bookkeeping for Jacobians;
the next lesson makes that precise.

<Callout kind="deep" title="Reading the loss surface">
  Each partial $\partial J / \partial \theta_i$ answers a question you
  actually care about: *if I nudge weight $i$ up by a tiny amount, how much
  does the loss change?* The gradient is that answer for every weight at
  once. On a contour plot of the loss, the gradient is the uphill arrow and
  the negative gradient the downhill one — always crossing contours at a
  right angle, never along them. Every optimizer you will ever run is a
  variation on "step along the negative gradient."
</Callout>

## A worked example: both partials

Let $f(x, y) = x^2 y + e^{xy}$.

With respect to $x$, holding $y$ fixed: the term $x^2 y$ differentiates by
the power rule to $2xy$; the term $e^{xy}$ differentiates by the chain rule
to $e^{xy} \cdot y$. So

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2xy + y\, e^{xy}
$$

With respect to $y$, holding $x$ fixed: $x^2 y$ is linear in $y$ and gives
$x^2$; $e^{xy}$ contributes $e^{xy} \cdot x$. So

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x^2 + x\, e^{xy}
$$

Evaluate at $(1, 1)$: the partials are $2 + e \approx 4.72$ and
$1 + e \approx 3.72$, so

$$
\nabla f(1, 1) \approx (4.72, \; 3.72), \qquad
\lVert \nabla f(1,1) \rVert \approx 6.0
$$

The surface rises in every direction from this point, and the fastest way
down is along the vector $(-4.72, \; -3.72)$.

## Worked problem

<Callout kind="info" title="Problem">
  Let $J(\theta_1, \theta_2) = (\theta_1 - 3)^2 + 2(\theta_2 + 1)^2$.
  (a) Compute $\nabla J$ and evaluate it at the origin. (b) Which single
  coordinate move of size $0.5$ reduces $J$ the most? (c) Give the unit
  direction of steepest descent.
</Callout>

<Solution>
  (a) $\nabla J = \big(2(\theta_1 - 3), \;\; 4(\theta_2 + 1)\big)$, so at the
  origin $\nabla J(0, 0) = (-6, \; 4)$. The loss there is $J = 9 + 2 = 11$.

  (b) Moving $\theta_1$ up by $0.5$: $J = (-2.5)^2 + 2 = 8.25$, a drop of
  $2.75$. Moving $\theta_2$ down by $0.5$: $J = 9 + 2(0.5)^2 = 9.5$, a drop
  of $1.5$. The $\theta_1$ move wins — matching its larger partial magnitude
  ($6$ versus $4$).

  (c) Steepest descent is $-\nabla J = (6, -4)$, with length
  $\sqrt{36 + 16} = \sqrt{52} \approx 7.21$. The unit direction is therefore

  $$
\frac{(6, -4)}{\sqrt{52}} \approx (0.83, \; -0.55)
  $$

  Notice the workflow: gradient, flip the sign, normalize. That is exactly
  what a gradient descent step does, just with the learning rate scaling the
  length instead.
</Solution>
