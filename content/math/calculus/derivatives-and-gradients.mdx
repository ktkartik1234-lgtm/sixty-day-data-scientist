Training a model is asking one question on repeat: *if I nudge this parameter
a little, does the loss get better or worse — and how fast?* The derivative
is the instrument that answers it. This track builds from that single idea to
the gradient that drives every optimizer you will ever run.

## The derivative is local sensitivity

For a function $f(x)$, the derivative at $x$ is the limit of average slopes
over shrinking windows:

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$$

Geometrically that is the slope of the tangent line, but the reading that
matters in ML is sensitivity. Rearranged, it is the first-order prediction:

$$f(x + h) \approx f(x) + f'(x)\,h$$

If the derivative at $x$ is $-2$, a tiny push of $+0.01$ on the input drops
the output by about $0.02$. A derivative of zero means the output is flat to
first order — you are standing at a candidate minimum or maximum.

## The three rules that do the work

Almost every loss and activation is assembled from powers, products and
compositions, so three rules cover most of what you will ever differentiate:

$$\frac{d}{dx}x^n = n\,x^{n-1} \qquad \text{(power rule)}$$

$$(f \cdot g)' = f'g + fg' \qquad \text{(product rule)}$$

$$(f \circ g)' = f'\big(g(x)\big)\cdot g'(x) \qquad \text{(chain rule)}$$

Quick sample: for $f(x) = x^2 \sin(x)$ the product rule gives
$f'(x) = 2x\sin(x) + x^2\cos(x)$. The chain rule is the deepest of the three —
it gets its own lesson at the end of this track, because it is the engine of
backpropagation.

## Many parameters: the gradient

Models do not have one parameter; they have thousands to billions. For a
function of many inputs, we differentiate with respect to each input while
holding the rest fixed — a *partial derivative* — and stack the results into
the **gradient**:

$$\nabla f = \begin{bmatrix}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1} \\[2pt]
\vdots \\[2pt]
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_d}
\end{bmatrix}$$

The gradient is a vector, so it has both magnitude and direction — and the
direction is special: $\nabla f$ points where $f$ increases fastest. Turn it
around and $-\nabla f$ points where $f$ *decreases* fastest. That single fact
is the entire theory of gradient descent, the subject of the next track.

The next lesson works partial derivatives in full detail; for now, treat the
gradient as one sensitivity number per parameter, collected into a vector.

## Trust, then verify: numerical gradients

Hand-derived gradients are where bugs live. The cheap, universal check is the
**central difference** approximation:

$$f'(x) \approx \frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h)}{2h}$$

It is accurate to order $h^2$: with $h = 10^{-5}$ you get roughly eight
correct digits, far more than needed to expose a wrong formula. In NumPy:

```python
import numpy as np

def f(w):                      # a toy "loss"
    return np.sin(3 * w) + w ** 2

def grad_analytic(w):          # your hand-derived formula
    return 3 * np.cos(3 * w) + 2 * w

h = 1e-5
w = 1.3
numeric = (f(w + h) - f(w - h)) / (2 * h)
print(grad_analytic(w), numeric)   # identical to about 8 decimals
```

If the analytic and numeric values disagree beyond the third decimal, your
derivative is wrong — not the numerics.

<Callout kind="deep" title="Where you'll meet it">
  This trick is called *gradient checking*, and it never went away. Frameworks
  verify their autograd engines against finite differences, and every custom
  layer you write during the neural-network days deserves the same treatment:
  compare the backward pass against a central-difference estimate before you
  trust a single training curve.
</Callout>

## Worked problem

<Callout kind="info" title="Problem">
  Let $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$. (a) Compute $f'(x)$ and find both critical points.
  (b) Classify each using the sign of $f''$. (c) Check $f'(2)$ against the
  central difference with $h = 0.01$.
</Callout>

<Solution>
  (a) By the power rule, $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3$. Setting it to zero gives
  $3x^2 = 3$, so $x = \pm 1$.

  (b) $f''(x) = 6x$. At $x = 1$: $f'' = 6$, positive — the curve bends
  upward, so $x = 1$ is a local minimum with $f(1) = -2$. At $x = -1$:
  $f'' = -6$, negative — a local maximum with $f(-1) = 2$.

  (c) $f'(2) = 3(4) - 3 = 9$. Numerically, $f(2.01) = 2.090601$ and
  $f(1.99) = 1.910599$, so

  $$\frac{f(2.01) - f(1.99)}{0.02} = \frac{0.180002}{0.02} = 9.0001$$

  The central difference lands within $10^{-4}$ of the analytic answer — the
  residual is the expected order-$h^2$ truncation error, not a bug.
</Solution>
