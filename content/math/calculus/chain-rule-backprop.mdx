A neural network is not one function. It is functions composed inside
functions — linear maps, activations, losses — stacked dozens of layers deep.
The chain rule is the tool that differentiates the whole stack, and
backpropagation is simply the chain rule organized to run efficiently.

## Composition is the whole game

Write $(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))$: first apply $g$, then apply $f$ to the
result. Logistic regression is already a composition — linear map, then
sigmoid, then loss:

$$
J = \underbrace{L}_{\text{loss}}\Big(\underbrace{\sigma}_{\text{sigmoid}}
\big(\underbrace{w^\top x + b}_{\text{linear}}\big)\Big)
$$

A deep network just keeps nesting:
$J = L\big(a_L(a_{L-1}(\cdots a_1(x)\cdots))\big)$. If we could not
differentiate compositions, we could not train any of this — so the chain
rule is not an optional topic; it is the price of admission.

## The chain rule

If $u = g(x)$ and $y = f(u)$, then

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx}
$$

In words: *local derivatives multiply along the path from input to output.*
For three composed stages $x \to z \to a \to J$:

$$
\frac{\partial J}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial J}{\partial a} \cdot
\frac{\partial a}{\partial z} \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}
$$

Each factor is the derivative of a *simple* function evaluated at a *known
point*. That is the whole trick: complicated derivative in, a product of
simple derivatives out.

## Computational graphs

Unroll the composition into a graph where every node is one elementary
operation. For $z = w \cdot x$ and $J = z^2$:

```
x ──┐
    ├──▶ z = w·x ──▶ J = z²
w ──┘
```

The forward pass computes every node and — crucially — *stores* the values.
To differentiate, walk the same graph backward, multiplying together the
local derivative of each node, each evaluated at the value that node actually
took during the forward pass.

## Backpropagation: the chain rule, run in reverse

Backprop is reverse-mode automatic differentiation: start at the output $J$
with gradient 1 and sweep backward, pushing products of local derivatives
through the graph. Two properties make it the workhorse of deep learning:

- **One backward pass yields every partial at once** — $\partial J /
  \partial w$ for all millions of weights simultaneously, at roughly the
  cost of one extra forward pass.
- The naive alternative does not scale: finite differences would need one
  full forward pass *per parameter*, which is hopeless beyond a handful.

## A two-node example, with numbers

Let $x = 2$, $w = 3$, so $z = wx = 6$ and $J = z^2 = 36$.

Backward, one local derivative at a time:
$\partial J / \partial z = 2z = 12$ and $\partial z / \partial w = x = 2$.
Multiply along the path:

$$
\frac{\partial J}{\partial w} = 12 \times 2 = 24
$$

Sanity check by collapsing the graph: $J(w) = (2w)^2 = 4w^2$, so
$dJ/dw = 8w = 24$ at $w = 3$. The graph and the closed form agree — as they
must, since they are the same theorem worn two ways.

<Callout kind="deep" title="What loss.backward() actually does">
  An autograd framework like PyTorch is bookkeeping for exactly this: it
  records the graph as you compute, stores activations during the forward
  pass, and multiplies local derivatives in reverse. The memory cost of
  training — caching activations — and the ability to drop in custom layers
  (supply a forward function and a local derivative) both fall straight out
  of this picture. There is no deeper magic underneath.
</Callout>

## Worked problem

<Callout kind="info" title="Problem">
  With $x = 2$ and $w = 2$, define $z = wx$, then $a = z^2$, then
  $J = (a - y)^2$ with $y = 0$. Compute $J$ and $\partial J / \partial w$
  through the graph, then verify against a closed form in $w$ alone.
</Callout>

<Solution>
  Forward: $z = 2 \times 2 = 4$, then $a = 4^2 = 16$, then
  $J = (16 - 0)^2 = 256$.

  Backward, one local derivative per edge:

  $$
\frac{\partial J}{\partial a} = 2(a - y) = 32, \qquad
    \frac{\partial a}{\partial z} = 2z = 8, \qquad
    \frac{\partial z}{\partial w} = x = 2
  $$

  Multiply along the path from $w$ to $J$:

  $$
\frac{\partial J}{\partial w} = 32 \times 8 \times 2 = 512
  $$

  Check by eliminating the intermediates: $z = 2w$, so
  $J = \big((2w)^2\big)^2 = 16w^4$ and $dJ/dw = 64w^3 = 64 \times 8 = 512$
  at $w = 2$. The reversed graph reproduces the closed form exactly — three
  cheap local derivatives standing in for one messy global one.
</Solution>
