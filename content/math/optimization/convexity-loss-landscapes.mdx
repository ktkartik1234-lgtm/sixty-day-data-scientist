Gradient descent is only guaranteed to work if the terrain cooperates.
Convexity is the precise way of saying the terrain is a single bowl, and it
draws a real line in practice: some models train reliably with almost no
tuning, while others demand every trick you know.

## Convex sets and convex functions

A set is convex if the line segment between any two of its points stays
inside the set. A function is convex if the same idea holds for its graph —
the chord between any two points lies on or above the curve:

$$f\big(\lambda x + (1-\lambda)\,y\big) \;\le\;
\lambda f(x) + (1-\lambda) f(y) \qquad \text{for all } \lambda \in [0, 1]$$

Blend two inputs, and the loss at the blend is no worse than the blended
losses. Quadratics like $x^2$, the absolute value and $e^x$ are convex;
anything that dips, rises and dips again is not. Linear functions count too —
the definition demands "on or above," and equality is allowed.

## One bowl, one bottom

Convexity collapses the optimization story into three clean statements:

- **Every local minimum is the global minimum.** Two separate bottoms would
  force the chord between them to dip below the curve, violating the
  definition — so there is nothing better hiding elsewhere.
- **Zero gradient is enough.** If $\nabla f(x^*) = 0$ and $f$ is convex, then
  $x^*$ is a global minimum. No second-order checks, no second-guessing.
- **Gradient descent converges** from any starting point, given a learning
  rate small enough for the curvature — the quadratic analysis of the last
  lesson generalizes to every convex loss.

## The convex classics

Linear regression with squared error is convex in the weights:

$$J(w) = \lVert Xw - y \rVert^2$$

an affine map composed with a squared norm — one bowl, which is why it even
admits a closed-form solution, the normal equation
$w = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y$. Logistic regression's cross-entropy is
convex in $w$ as well: train it twice from different initializations and you
land on the same answer, which is exactly why it makes such a dependable
baseline in the modeling days. The hinge loss behind SVMs is convex too.
The fine print matters, though: convex *in the parameters* — a claim about
the geometry you optimize over, not about your data.

## Deep networks are non-convex — and it barely matters

Stack two linear layers and the loss is already non-convex in the weights.
Worse, the landscape is full of structure: swap two hidden units and the
network computes an identical function — a permutation symmetry — so
equivalent minima come in entire connected families. And in high dimensions,
random critical points are overwhelmingly likely to be **saddle points**
rather than minima: for a minimum, *every* curvature direction must be
positive, and with thousands of independent directions that is exponentially
unlikely (Dauphin et al., 2014).

The saving grace, established empirically over years: good-enough minima are
everywhere, and the global minimum is frequently the overfit one anyway.
Training a deep network is not a hunt for the lowest point — it is a hunt for
a basin that generalizes.

<Callout kind="deep" title="Reading the terrain from the loss curve">
  A long flat stretch followed by a sudden drop is the signature of a saddle
  or plateau being escaped — not of a bug. It is also why "stuck" rarely
  means "trapped in a local minimum" on modern networks: with noisy
  mini-batches, saddles shake loose given steps big enough to roll off.
  On convex problems the loss curve is boring on purpose; when it is not
  boring on a deep net, reach for learning-rate and momentum tools before
  questioning the architecture.
</Callout>

## What this means in practice

- **Convex problems:** the solver does the work. Few knobs, initialization
  does not matter, and convergence is a property of the problem, not of your
  patience.
- **Non-convex problems:** initialization, learning-rate schedule, batch
  size and momentum become design decisions, because they decide *which
  basin* you fall into.
- **In both cases, the loss curve is your map** — flatness, oscillation and
  cliffs all mean something specific about the terrain underneath.

## Worked problem

<Callout kind="info" title="Problem">
  Two candidate losses: $J_A(w_1, w_2) = w_1^2 + 10\,w_2^2$ and
  $J_B(w_1, w_2) = w_1^2 - w_2^2$. Use the Hessian at the origin to classify
  each critical point, and connect the eigenvalues to convexity.
</Callout>

<Solution>
  Both gradients vanish at the origin, so both are critical points. The
  Hessians (the matrix of second partials) are constant here:

  $$H_A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 20 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
    H_B = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$$

  $H_A$ has eigenvalues $2$ and $20$ — both positive, so the origin is a
  minimum. Since the eigenvalues are positive *everywhere*, $J_A$ is strictly
  convex and that minimum is the unique global one.

  $H_B$ has eigenvalues $+2$ and $-2$: the origin is a **saddle point**.
  Descend along $w_1$ and you climb along $w_2$ — the eigenvectors of the
  Hessian (the linear algebra track again) give the directions, the
  eigenvalues the curvature in each.

  The general lesson: convexity is exactly the statement that the Hessian
  never has a negative eigenvalue in any direction — no ridges, only bowls.
</Solution>
