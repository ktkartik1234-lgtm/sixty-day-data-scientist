Calculus hands you a direction; optimization turns it into an algorithm.
Gradient descent is that algorithm — a few lines of arithmetic that, applied
persistently, train everything from linear regression to billion-parameter
language models.

## The update rule

$$\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta\, \nabla J(\theta_t)$$

Read it right to left: at the current parameters $\theta_t$, compute the
gradient of the loss, then step a small amount $\eta$ — the **learning rate**
— in the *opposite* direction. Downhill, since $\nabla J$ points uphill.
Repeat until the loss stops improving. In one dimension it is barely anything
at all:

$$\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\, J'(\theta)$$

Everything else in this track — momentum, adaptivity, schedules — is a
refinement of this one line.

## The learning rate dominates everything

Take the toy loss $J(\theta) = \theta^2$ with $J'(\theta) = 2\theta$. The
update collapses to:

$$\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - 2\eta\,\theta_t = (1 - 2\eta)\,\theta_t$$

Every step multiplies the distance to the optimum by $|1 - 2\eta|$, which
sorts all behavior into four regimes:

- Small $\eta$ (say $0.1$): smooth geometric convergence — safe but slow.
- $\eta = 0.5$: one perfect step straight to the bottom.
- Between $0.5$ and $1$: overshoot and oscillate, but still shrink.
- Above $1$: every step makes things worse — divergence.

Real networks tell the same story with extra noise, which is why the learning
rate is the first hyperparameter anyone tunes and the last one they stop
tuning.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Reading a loss curve">
  Loss exploding or producing NaN? Lower the learning rate first. Crawling
  down almost imperceptibly? Raise it. Sawtoothing with no trend? The
  iterate is oscillating across a valley floor — lower it or add momentum.
  Before tuning anything fancier, sweep the rate on a log scale: one run per
  decade from $10^{-5}$ up to $10^{-1}$ tells you which regime you are in.
</Callout>

## Batch, mini-batch or stochastic

The gradient in the update rule is an average over data, and how you estimate
it defines three regimes:

| Variant | Gradient from | Per-step cost | Noise |
|---|---|---|---|
| Batch | all $n$ examples | high | none |
| Mini-batch | a chunk of 32–1024 | moderate | some |
| Stochastic | a single example | tiny | lots |

Mini-batch is the working default: cheap enough to take many steps per epoch,
accurate enough to descend. The noise is not pure downside — it jitters the
trajectory out of shallow traps and acts as a mild regularizer. Full batch
buys an exact gradient you rarely need; single-example stochastic descent is
mostly of historical and theoretical interest.

## Where descent gets stuck

Non-convex losses — the geometry of the next lesson — add terrain beyond
smooth bowls. **Saddle points**, flat in some directions while curving up in
others, are the common failure mode in high dimensions, far more common than
true local minima. **Plateaus** of tiny gradients make progress crawl.

A minimal saddle: $J(w_1, w_2) = w_1^2 - w_2^2$ has zero gradient at the
origin, so plain descent stops there — yet it is no minimum. Nudge the
iterate to $(0.1, \; 0)$ and the next step slides back toward the valley in
$w_1$ but *away* along $w_2$: the update
$\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \nabla J$ actively climbs the $-w_2^2$ term.
The standard defenses are exactly this kind of jitter — noisy mini-batches,
momentum and adaptive step sizes — the subject of the final lesson in this
track.

## Worked problem

<Callout kind="info" title="Problem">
  Minimize $J(\theta) = \theta^2$ by gradient descent from $\theta_0 = 3$
  with learning rate $\eta = 0.1$. (a) Compute two full steps and the loss
  after each. (b) Describe the pattern and estimate how many steps to get
  within $0.01$ of the optimum. (c) What does $\eta = 1$ do instead?
</Callout>

<Solution>
  The derivative is $J'(\theta) = 2\theta$.

  (a) Step 1: gradient $= 2 \times 3 = 6$, so
  $\theta_1 = 3 - 0.1 \times 6 = 2.4$ and $J(\theta_1) = 5.76$.
  Step 2: gradient $= 2 \times 2.4 = 4.8$, so
  $\theta_2 = 2.4 - 0.1 \times 4.8 = 1.92$ and $J(\theta_2) = 3.6864$.

  (b) Each step multiplies the distance to the optimum by
  $1 - 2(0.1) = 0.8$, so $\theta_t = 3 \times 0.8^t$ — geometric convergence,
  with the loss shrinking by a factor of $0.64$ per step. Reaching a distance
  of $0.01$ needs

  $$t \approx \frac{\ln(0.01/3)}{\ln(0.8)} \approx 26 \text{ steps}$$

  (c) At $\eta = 1$ the update becomes $\theta_{t+1} = -\theta_t$: the
  iterate bounces between $3$ and $-3$ forever. That is the oscillation
  regime — one notch above divergence, and the classic signature of a rate
  set too high for the curvature.
</Solution>
