Correlation is the cheapest signal in data science: one line of NumPy gives
you a number between -1 and 1 describing how two variables move together.
The expensive lesson is what that number does *not* say — and how much
engineering effort, from randomized experiments to careful feature selection,
exists to compensate for exactly that gap.

## Pearson correlation

For paired observations $(x_i, y_i)$, the Pearson correlation coefficient is
the covariance of $x$ and $y$, normalized by both standard deviations:

$$r = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}
{\sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2}\,\sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \bar{y})^2}}
= \frac{\widehat{\text{cov}}(x, y)}{s_x \, s_y}$$

Facts worth memorizing: $r \in [-1, 1]$; $|r| = 1$ means the points lie
exactly on a straight line; $r = 0$ means *no linear association* — which is
not the same as "no relationship" (a perfect $y = x^2$ arc scores near zero).
And $r$ is famously fragile: a single extreme outlier can move it
dramatically in either direction. For simple linear regression there is a
tidy identity: $r^2 = R^2$, the fraction of variance the line explains.

## What correlation does not say

Correlation is symmetric and silent about mechanism. $r \approx 0.9$ between
ice cream sales and drowning deaths does not mean ice cream is dangerous; it
means both rise together. Four different worlds produce identical
correlations:

1. $x$ causes $y$;
2. $y$ causes $x$ (reverse causation — think firefighting crews vs fire damage);
3. a third variable $z$ causes both (a **confounder**);
4. pure coincidence, especially in small samples.

The correlation coefficient cannot distinguish among them. Only information
from outside the dataset — how the data was generated — can.

## Confounders

A **confounder** is a variable that influences both the feature and the
target, manufacturing an association between two things that have no direct
link. Weather drives both ice cream sales and swimming; both drive the
correlation. Classic ML examples: hospital patients look sicker than healthy
people *despite* treatment (only sick people get treated), and in hiring
data, neighborhood zip codes correlate with outcomes only through wealth and
opportunity they proxy. Confounding is why observational correlations
routinely evaporate — or reverse — under scrutiny.

## Simpson's paradox

The sharpest warning: an association can **reverse** when you split the data
by a hidden group variable. Here is a job-training program evaluated on
400 people:

| Group | Enrolled | Not enrolled |
|---|---|---|
| Low-skill participants | 40/160 employed (25%) | 8/40 employed (20%) |
| High-skill participants | 36/40 employed (90%) | 128/160 employed (80%) |
| **Overall** | **76/200 (38%)** | **136/200 (68%)** |

Read the bottom row and the program looks harmful: enrollees are employed at
38% versus 68%. Read the strata and the program helps *twice over* — better
outcomes for both low-skill (25% vs 20%) and high-skill (90% vs 80%)
participants. The reversal happens because enrollment and employment share a
confounder: the enrolled group is dominated by low-skill participants (160
of 200), while the non-enrolled group is dominated by high-skill participants
(160 of 200) — and skill level alone drives employment either way. The
overall rates compare two different populations, not two treatments.

## Why randomized experiments win

Randomization severs the confounder's grip. In an A/B test, the coin flip
that assigns each user to treatment or control is — by construction —
independent of age, intent, device, and every hidden variable. Any
confounder gets balanced across groups in expectation, so a significant
difference in outcomes measures the treatment's causal effect, not the
groups' pre-existing differences. This is why the experiment is the gold
standard, and why the t-test and CLT machinery from earlier in this track
exist: they quantify whether a randomized difference exceeds noise.

Observational data (no coin flip) demands humility instead:

- **Feature selection is not causal analysis.** A feature with high
  correlation to the target can be pure confounding proxy — useful for
  *prediction*, meaningless as a *lever*. A model may legitimately use a
  zip code to predict default while the company must never act on it.
- **Control where you can:** stratify, match, or include confounders as
  covariates — and know that regression adjustment fixes only the
  confounders you thought of.
- **Beware post-treatment bias:** dropping or controlling for variables
  downstream of the treatment can erase the very effect you seek.

<Callout kind="warn" title="Connection to ML">
  A feature-selection pipeline that ranks features by correlation will
  happily promote confounded proxies — and a model trained on them will
  learn the world as it was, not the effect of changing it. Prediction and
  intervention are different questions; correlation answers the first,
  randomization answers the second.
</Callout>

## Worked problem

<Callout kind="info" title="Problem">
  Your team observes that users who enable two-factor authentication are
  three times less likely to be hacked, and proposes forcing it on everyone.
  Before running that experiment, show with a small two-by-two table how a
  confounder (security-mindedness) could produce this correlation with
  no causal effect of 2FA at all.
</Callout>

<Solution>
  Suppose security-minded users are far likelier to enable 2FA **and** far
  likelier to avoid risky links and reuse passwords — which alone prevents
  hacks. Split 1,000 users by mindset; hacked rates by cell:

  | Mindset | 2FA on | 2FA off |
  |---|---|---|
  | Security-minded | 6 of 300 hacked (2%) | 2 of 100 hacked (2%) |
  | Casual | 40 of 100 hacked (40%) | 200 of 500 hacked (40%) |
  | **Overall** | **46 of 400 (11.5%)** | **202 of 600 (33.7%)** |

  The bottom row reproduces the observed story: 2FA users are hacked at
  11.5% versus 33.7% — roughly three times less likely. But within *each*
  mindset class the hacked rate is exactly equal (2% and 2%, 40% and 40%):
  2FA itself changes nothing.

  The correlation exists only because 2FA adoption is concentrated among
  security-minded users (300 of 400, versus 100 of 600), and mindset alone
  protects them. Forcing 2FA on everyone would change nothing — a randomized
  rollout would have exposed this immediately, while the observational
  correlation confidently recommends a useless policy.
</Solution>
