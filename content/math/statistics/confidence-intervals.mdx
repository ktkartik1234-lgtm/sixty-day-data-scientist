A validation accuracy of 0.87 sounds like a fact. It is actually one draw
from a distribution of accuracies you could have gotten with a different
sample of data. A **confidence interval** wraps that single number in an
honest account of the uncertainty — and reading it correctly requires
unlearning a very natural misreading.

## What a confidence interval actually is

The correct interpretation:

> If you repeated the data collection many times and built a 95% interval
> the same way each time, about 95% of those intervals would contain the
> true value.

The **procedure** is reliable; any **single interval** either contains the
truth or does not, and you never find out which. The classic misreadings
flip this:

- "There is a 95% probability the true mean lies in *this* interval." No —
  the true value is fixed, not random; the randomness was in your sample.
- "95% of the data lies inside this interval." That is a prediction interval,
  a much wider object.
- "A 99% interval is better, so I will always use one." Wider intervals buy
  confidence with precision — you may end up saying nothing useful.

## A confidence interval for a mean

For a sample of size $n$ with mean $\bar{x}$ and standard deviation $s$, the
CLT plus a t-critical value give:

$$\bar{x} \;\pm\; t^{*}_{n-1} \times \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

Concretely: 36 customers spend $\bar{x} = 42$ dollars with $s = 12$. Then
$\text{SE} = 12/6 = 2$, and with 35 degrees of freedom $t^{*} \approx 2.03$:

$$42 \pm 2.03 \times 2 \;=\; (37.9,\; 46.1) \text{ dollars}$$

The knob-turning is now familiar: intervals narrow like $1/\sqrt{n}$, widen
with the $t^{*}$ you demand (95% vs 99%), and widen with the inherent
spread $s$ of the data.

## Bootstrap confidence intervals

What if the statistic is ugly — a median, an AUC, a difference of
accuracies — and no textbook formula exists? **Bootstrap** to the rescue:
approximate the sampling distribution by resampling *your own data* with
replacement, recomputing the statistic each time:

```text
given fitted model predictions and true labels y on n = 1000 test points:
B = 2000
for b in 1..B:
    idx = draw n indices 0..n-1 uniformly WITH replacement
    acc[b] = accuracy(y[idx], yhat[idx])      # same model, resampled data
interval = percentiles of acc at 2.5% and 97.5%
```

No distributional assumptions, no formulas — just the empirical sampling
distribution of your metric. For accuracy on a small holdout set this is
often the only honest interval you can get, and it routinely reveals that
"a 2-point improvement" is well inside the noise.

## Why intervals beat point estimates

Two models scoring 0.871 and 0.865 on a 500-row test set are effectively
tied — the bootstrap intervals overlap almost completely — yet the point
estimates alone will make you pick one and defend it. Intervals convert
false precision into calibrated decisions:

- **Model comparison:** overlap means "not distinguishable on this data",
  which saves you from chasing noise.
- **Deployment risk:** an accuracy of 0.87 with interval (0.82, 0.92) warns
  the worst plausible case may be unacceptable.
- **Progress reporting:** an improvement inside its own interval is not a
  headline yet.

<Callout kind="deep" title="Connection to ML">
  Leaderboards and papers report a single test-set number because intervals
  look indecisive. Professional practice is the opposite: quote the interval,
  especially when the test set is small (capstone-style evaluation, days
  55–60, will punish you for anything else). A point estimate without an
  uncertainty claim is a hypothesis, not a result.
</Callout>

## Worked problem

<Callout kind="info" title="Problem">
  Your classifier scores a mean F1 of $\bar{x} = 0.81$ across 25 repeated
  evaluation splits, with standard deviation $s = 0.10$ across splits.
  Build the 95% confidence interval for the true mean F1, then judge a rival
  model whose mean F1 is 0.84 on the same 25 splits.
</Callout>

<Solution>
  The standard error of the mean F1 is

  $$\text{SE} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{0.10}{\sqrt{25}} = 0.02$$

  With $df = 24$ the 95% t-critical value is $t^{*} \approx 2.064$, so

  $$0.81 \pm 2.064 \times 0.02 \;=\; 0.81 \pm 0.041 \;=\; (0.769,\; 0.851)$$

  The rival's 0.84 sits *inside* your interval, so on 25 splits these models
  are not statistically distinguishable — switching is a coin flip, not an
  upgrade. To separate two models 3 points apart with $s = 0.10$ you would
  need roughly $n \approx (2.064 \times 0.10 / 0.03)^2 \approx 48$ splits.
  Had we used a bootstrap over test points instead, the logic is identical:
  resample, rescore, take the 2.5% and 97.5% percentiles.
</Solution>
