The CLT tells you sample means are noisy. Hypothesis testing tells you how
much noise — enough to answer the question every experiment, feature and
model change eventually asks: *is this difference real, or did randomness
hand it to me?*

## The null and alternative hypotheses

Every test stages a courtroom drama between two claims:

- $H_0$ (null): there is no effect — the treatment does nothing, the new
  model is the same as the old one. The default you hold until evidence
  convicts it.
- $H_1$ (alternative): there is an effect — what you usually hoped was true.

You never prove $H_0$; you either *reject* it (evidence against it is strong)
or *fail to reject* it (evidence is weak — which is not the same as evidence
of absence). The word "significant" has a precise meaning here: unlikely
under $H_0$. It does not mean important, large, or useful.

## Test statistics and p-values

A **test statistic** condenses your data into one number that measures how
far the data sits from what $H_0$ predicts, in units of its own noise:

$$t = \frac{\text{estimate} - \text{hypothesized value}}{\text{standard error}}$$

The **p-value** is the probability — computed *assuming* $H_0$ is true — of
seeing a statistic at least this extreme:

$$p = P\!\left(\text{data at least this extreme} \;\middle|\; H_0\right)$$

Read the conditioning carefully: the p-value is **not** the probability that
$H_0$ is true, and not the probability the result happened by chance. It
answers "if there were truly no effect, how surprising is my sample?" — a
small $p$ means the no-effect story struggles to explain your data.

## A t-test, end to end with numbers

A vendor claims their inference endpoint averages 12 ms latency. You time
$n = 36$ requests and get $\bar{x} = 11.3$ ms with $s = 1.5$ ms. Faster —
but is 0.7 ms beyond noise?

1. Hypotheses: $H_0: \mu = 12$ vs $H_1: \mu \ne 12$, significance level
   $\alpha = 0.05$.
2. Standard error: $\text{SE} = s/\sqrt{n} = 1.5/6 = 0.25$ ms.
3. Statistic: $t = (11.3 - 12)/0.25 = -2.8$, with $n - 1 = 35$ degrees of
   freedom.
4. A t-distribution with 35 degrees of freedom puts only about $p \approx 0.008$
   of its probability at $\lvert t \rvert \ge 2.8$.

Since $p = 0.008$ is below $\alpha = 0.05$, reject $H_0$: the endpoint really
is faster, and it is not a fluke of your 36 requests. Equivalently, the
observed $t = -2.8$ sits beyond the 2.5% critical value of roughly
$\pm 2.03$.

## Type I and type II errors

Two ways to be wrong, with asymmetric costs:

| | Reject $H_0$ | Fail to reject $H_0$ |
|---|---|---|
| $H_0$ true | **Type I error** (false alarm), rate $\alpha$ | correct |
| $H_0$ false | correct (probability $1-\beta$) | **Type II error** (miss), rate $\beta$ |

**Statistical power** is $1 - \beta$: the probability your test detects an
effect that truly exists. Power grows with sample size, with the size of the
effect, and with how much you tolerate false alarms ($\alpha$). Underpowered
tests are the quiet killer of applied work — you run a real improvement, get
a noisy "no significant difference", and ship the status quo.

<Callout kind="warn" title="Connection to ML">
  Feature selection is a multiple-comparisons minefield. Screen 100 features
  for correlation with the target at $\alpha = 0.05$ and, even if *none*
  matter, about five will look significant by pure chance — features that are
  noise with a p-value costume. Control this with Bonferroni
  ($\alpha/m$ per test) or, better, validate selected features on data they
  were never selected on.
</Callout>

## The multiple-comparisons trap

Run one test at $\alpha = 0.05$ and you accept a 5% false-alarm rate. Run 20
independent tests and the chance of *at least one* false alarm is

$$P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - (0.95)^{20} \approx 0.64$$

Sixty-four percent. A/B platforms that track dozens of metrics, and
researchers who test twenty hyperparameter choices, are testing many
hypotheses at once — one of them will "win" eventually. Bonferroni's fix is
brutally simple: test each hypothesis at $\alpha/m$ for $m$ tests, buying
protection at the cost of power.

## Worked problem

<Callout kind="info" title="Problem">
  You add a feature to your credit-risk model and evaluate both versions on
  the same 25 cross-validation folds. The per-fold improvement has mean
  $0.9$ percentage points with standard deviation $s = 2.0$ points. Test at
  $\alpha = 0.05$ whether the feature helps, and state the type I and type
  II risks in this decision.
</Callout>

<Solution>
  Treat the 25 per-fold improvements as one sample and test
  $H_0: \mu = 0$ (no real improvement) with a one-sample t-test.

  $$\text{SE} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{2.0}{\sqrt{25}} = 0.4$$

  $$t = \frac{0.9 - 0}{0.4} = 2.25, \quad df = 24$$

  The two-sided critical value at $\alpha = 0.05$ with 24 degrees of freedom
  is about $2.064$. Since $2.25$ exceeds it (corresponding to
  $p \approx 0.034$), reject $H_0$: the improvement is distinguishable from
  fold-to-fold noise.

  Type I risk: the feature actually does nothing and you adopted it anyway —
  capped at 5% by design. Type II risk: the feature helps but 25 folds are
  too noisy to prove it, and you wrongly discard it. With $s = 2.0$ and
  $n = 25$, only improvements of roughly $2.064 \times 0.4 \approx 0.8$
  points or more are detectable — anything smaller is in the power blind
  spot.
</Solution>
