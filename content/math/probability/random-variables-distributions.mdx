A random variable is a number attached to an uncertain outcome: one if a user
clicks, zero if they do not; the milliseconds a request waits; the count of
errors in the next hour. A distribution describes which values show up and how
often — and nearly every loss function, evaluation metric, and noise assumption
in ML is built from a handful of these.

## Discrete versus continuous

A **discrete** random variable takes countable values, and its probabilities
live in the PMF: $p(x_i) = P(X = x_i)$, with every value's mass summing to
one. A **continuous** random variable takes values on a continuum; the
probability of any exact value is zero, so we speak of a density $f(x)$
instead, and only areas over intervals carry probability.

The distinction is practical, not pedantic: counts get discrete models,
measurements get continuous ones, and picking the wrong family is a modeling
error that no amount of hyperparameter tuning repairs.

## PMF, PDF, CDF

The cumulative distribution function $F(x) = P(X \le x)$ is the common
language of both worlds:

| Object | Discrete | Continuous |
|---|---|---|
| Probability at a point | $p(x) = P(X = x)$ | $f(x)$ is a density, $P(X = x) = 0$ |
| Total mass | $\sum_i p(x_i) = 1$ | $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1$ |
| CDF | $F(x) = \sum_{x_i \le x} p(x_i)$ | $F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f(t)\,dt$ |
| Recover from the CDF | $p(x_i) = F(x_i) - F(x_{i-1})$ | $f(x) = F'(x)$ |

## The four you will meet weekly

| Distribution | Models | Law | ML use-case |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bernoulli($p$) | one yes-or-no trial | $P(X = 1) = p$ | did the user click? |
| Binomial($n, p$) | $n$ independent trials | $P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}$ | conversions out of $n$ visitors |
| Poisson($\lambda$) | counts per interval | $P(X = k) = \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}$ | requests or frauds per hour |
| Normal($\mu, \sigma^2$) | sums of small effects | $f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}$ | measurement noise, weight init |

Each family carries a few parameters you will spend a career estimating, and
its mean and variance come free with them: the Bernoulli mean is exactly $p$;
the Binomial has mean $np$ and variance $np(1-p)$; the Poisson has mean and
variance both equal to $\lambda$ — high variance counts are its signature;
and the Normal holds about 68% of its mass within one standard deviation of
$\mu$ and 95% within two. Fitting a distribution to data means estimating
these parameters, and most of classical statistics follows from there.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Why the Normal is everywhere">
  Add up many small, roughly independent effects — sensor noise, rounding,
  dozens of weak features — and the sum lands on a bell curve no matter what
  the pieces looked like. That is the Central Limit Theorem peeking through,
  and it is why Gaussian noise assumptions and Gaussian weight initializations
  saturate machine learning. (Full treatment in a later statistics lesson.)
</Callout>

## Sampling in NumPy

Every experiment you will run starts here:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=42)

clicks = rng.binomial(n=1000, p=0.02, size=10)   # conversions per campaign
arrivals = rng.poisson(lam=4.0, size=10)          # support tickets per hour
noise = rng.normal(loc=0.0, scale=0.1, size=3)    # measurement noise
coin = rng.random(5) < 0.5                        # Bernoulli via uniform draws
```

Always draw from a seeded generator: reproducibility is a feature, not a
preference.

Sampling is also the fastest way to check your own algebra. Draw a million
samples and compare the empirical moments with the closed-form formulas:

```python
sim = rng.binomial(5, 0.3, size=1_000_000)
print(sim.mean(), sim.var())   # about 1.5 and 1.05, i.e. np and np(1-p)
```

## Worked problem

<Callout kind="info" title="Problem">
  Your landing page converts with probability $p = 0.3$. Out of the next
  5 visitors, what is the probability that exactly 2 convert? That 2 or fewer
  convert?
</Callout>

<Solution>
  With $X \sim \text{Binomial}(5,\, 0.3)$, the PMF gives:

  $$P(X = 2) = \binom{5}{2} (0.3)^2 (0.7)^3 = 10 \times 0.09 \times 0.343 = 0.3087$$

  For the CDF, sum the PMF over $k = 0, 1, 2$:

  $$P(X \le 2) = (0.7)^5 + 5(0.3)(0.7)^4 + 0.3087 \approx 0.168 + 0.360 + 0.309 = 0.837$$

  So 2 or fewer conversions happens about 84% of the time, even though the
  expected count is only $np = 1.5$ (the single most likely outcome is 1, with
  probability about 0.360). Sanity check in NumPy: after
  `rng.binomial(5, 0.3, size=100000)`, the sample mean should land close to
  1.5 and the fraction equal to 2 close to 0.309.
</Solution>
