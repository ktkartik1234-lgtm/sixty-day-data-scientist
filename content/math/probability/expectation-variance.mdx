A model's expected loss, an A/B test's lift, a portfolio's return — all are
expectations. But an expectation compresses an entire distribution into one
number, and the compression is where careless analysis goes to die.

## Expectation: the long-run average

For a discrete random variable with PMF $p(x_i)$:

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{i} x_i \, p(x_i)$$

and for continuous variables the sum becomes an integral over the density. The
same recipe weights any function of the outcome:

$$\mathbb{E}[g(X)] = \sum_{i} g(x_i)\, p(x_i)$$

This is exactly how expected loss works: $g$ is the loss function, $p$ is the
data distribution, and training is trying to push that weighted average down.

You never know $p$ exactly — you estimate. The sample mean of $n$ draws is the
Monte Carlo estimate of $\mathbb{E}[X]$, and the law of large numbers says it
converges as $n$ grows. Every validation metric you have ever printed is one
of these estimates pretending to be the real thing.

## Linearity of expectation

The most useful property in applied probability:

$$\mathbb{E}[aX + bY] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X] + b\,\mathbb{E}[Y]$$

It holds unconditionally — no independence required. Sum a thousand noisy
estimates and the expected total is the sum of the expected parts, which is
why ensemble averages, minibatch gradients, and Monte Carlo estimates all
behave predictably even when their pieces are entangled.

## Variance and standard deviation

Expectation says where the center sits; variance says how much the mass
spreads around it:

$$\text{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\left[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2\right] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \mathbb{E}[X]^2$$

Variance squares the units, so we usually quote the standard deviation
$\sigma = \sqrt{\text{Var}(X)}$ instead. Unlike expectation, variance is not
linear: $\text{Var}(aX) = a^2\,\text{Var}(X)$, and for independent variables
the variances add. One consequence powers all of statistics: average $n$
independent copies of $X$ and the variance of the average shrinks to
$\sigma^2 / n$, so its standard deviation — the standard error — falls like
$\sigma / \sqrt{n}$. That square root is why confidence intervals shrink so
slowly as data accumulates.

## Covariance and correlation

Covariance extends the idea to a pair of variables:

$$\text{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}\left[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y])\right]$$

positive when the two rise together, negative when one rises as the other
falls. Pearson correlation normalizes it to the range $[-1, 1]$ by dividing by
both standard deviations. Two warnings worth keeping: correlation is blind to
non-linear relationships (a perfect U-shape can score near zero), and
correlation never establishes causation — confounders produce beautiful
correlations all day long.

Stack features into a random vector and all pairwise covariances arrange
themselves into a matrix:

$$\Sigma = \mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu})(\mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top\right]$$

which is precisely the covariance matrix that PCA eigendecomposes in the
linear algebra track — the two lessons are one idea seen from both sides.

<Callout kind="deep" title="The mean alone deceives">
  Dataset A always loses 10 dollars; dataset B loses nothing or 20 dollars
  with equal chance. Identical means, wildly different risk. Model evaluation
  has the same shape: a validation score is a sample mean, and its variance
  across folds and seeds tells you whether that mean deserves trust at all.
  Report spread next to every average — loss plus standard deviation, mean
  plus confidence interval — or you are publishing half a statistic.
</Callout>

## Worked problem

<Callout kind="info" title="Problem">
  A fair six-sided die pays you the face value in dollars. Compute
  $\mathbb{E}[X]$, $\text{Var}(X)$, and the standard deviation of the payout.
</Callout>

<Solution>
  Each face has probability $p = 1/6$, so the expected payout is:

  $$\mathbb{E}[X] = \frac{1}{6}(1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6) = \frac{21}{6} = 3.5$$

  For the variance, use the computational form
  $\text{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \mathbb{E}[X]^2$. First:

  $$\mathbb{E}[X^2] = \frac{1}{6}(1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 + 36) = \frac{91}{6} \approx 15.167$$

  then subtract the squared mean:

  $$\text{Var}(X) = \frac{91}{6} - (3.5)^2 = \frac{91}{6} - \frac{49}{4} = \frac{182 - 147}{12} = \frac{35}{12} \approx 2.917$$

  The standard deviation is $\sqrt{35/12} \approx 1.708$ dollars. Interpretation:
  the long-run average payout is 3.5 dollars, but a single roll typically
  lands about 1.7 dollars away from it — which is exactly why one sample mean
  is never the whole story.
</Solution>
