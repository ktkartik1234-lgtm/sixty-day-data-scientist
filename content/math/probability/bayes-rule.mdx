## Conditional probability

The probability of $A$ given that $B$ happened:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$$

Everything downstream — precision, recall, likelihoods — is conditional
probability wearing different clothes.

## Bayes' rule

$$P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H) \, P(H)}{P(E)}$$

| Term | Name | In ML terms |
|---|---|---|
| $P(H \mid E)$ | Posterior | what the model predicts after seeing data |
| $P(E \mid H)$ | Likelihood | how well the hypothesis explains the data |
| $P(H)$ | Prior | what we believed before the data |
| $P(E)$ | Evidence | a normalizing constant |

<Callout kind="tip" title="Intuition">
  Bayes' rule is belief accounting: start from a prior, let the likelihood
  push you toward or away from the hypothesis, land on a posterior. Naive
  Bayes classifiers apply it directly; maximum-likelihood training is the
  special case of a flat prior.
</Callout>

## The base-rate trap

A test detects a disease affecting 1% of a population with 90% sensitivity and
a 5% false-positive rate. What is $P(\text{disease} \mid \text{positive})$?

$$P(D \mid +) = \frac{0.9 \times 0.01}{0.9 \times 0.01 + 0.05 \times 0.99} \approx 0.154$$

Only ~15%. The tiny prior dominates — and this exact trap reappears whenever
you evaluate a classifier on imbalanced data (capstone days 55–60).

## Worked problem

<Callout kind="info" title="Problem">
  A spam filter flags messages containing the word "free". 40% of spam contains
  it, 2% of ham does, and 30% of your inbox is spam. Compute the probability a
  flagged message is spam.
</Callout>

<Solution>
  $$P(\text{spam} \mid \text{free}) = \frac{0.4 \times 0.3}{0.4 \times 0.3 + 0.02 \times 0.7} = \frac{0.12}{0.134} \approx 0.896$$

  About 90% — the 20:1 likelihood ratio overwhelms the roughly 2:1 prior.
  Notice how the answer changes if spam drops to 5% of the inbox: the posterior
  falls to ≈65%. Priors always matter.
</Solution>
