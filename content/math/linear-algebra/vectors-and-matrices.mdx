Everything in machine learning is a tensor in motion. A dataset with $n$ rows
and $d$ features **is** a matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$; a linear
model's prediction **is** a matrix-vector product.

## Vectors: data points with geometry

A vector $\mathbf{x} = [x_1, \dots, x_d]^\top$ is one example described by $d$
numbers. Two operations carry all the meaning:

$$
\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{y} = \sum_{i=1}^{d} x_i y_i
\qquad \text{(dot product — similarity)}
$$

$$
\lVert \mathbf{x} \rVert_2 = \sqrt{\mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{x}}
\qquad \text{(L2 norm — magnitude)}
$$

The dot product measures *alignment*. Cosine similarity normalizes it:

$$
\cos(\theta) = \frac{\mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{y}}{\lVert \mathbf{x}\rVert \, \lVert \mathbf{y}\rVert}
$$

<Callout kind="deep" title="Where you'll meet it">
  Cosine similarity over embeddings is the engine of retrieval-augmented
  generation (Day 37) and every vector database you'll ever use.
</Callout>

## Matrices: transformations

Multiplying by a matrix transforms space. For a linear model:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{y}} = X\mathbf{w} + b
$$

each row of $X$ flows through the same transformation — which is why vectorized
NumPy code beats Python loops by orders of magnitude.

## Eigenvectors: the directions that matter

For a square matrix $A$, an eigenvector $\mathbf{v}$ satisfies:

$$
A\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}
$$

the transformation only stretches it, doesn't rotate it. PCA (Day 21) works
by finding the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix — the directions where
your data varies most.

## Worked problem

<Callout kind="info" title="Problem">
  Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$. Show that
  $\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ is an eigenvector and find
  its eigenvalue.
</Callout>

<Solution>
  $$
A\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2+1 \\ 1+2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = 3\,\mathbf{v}
  $$

  So $\lambda = 3$: the all-ones direction gets stretched threefold. The other
  eigenvector is $[1, -1]^\top$ with $\lambda = 1$.
</Solution>
