By 2016, sequence modeling had a ceiling, and everyone felt it. LSTMs were
the best tool available — and still couldn't be scaled the way the data
demanded. The Transformer didn't beat recurrence incrementally; it removed
the constraint that made scaling impossible.

## The three failures of recurrence

**1. No parallelism.** An RNN must process token 50 after token 49 — state
flows sequentially through time. GPUs idle while waiting for a chain of small
dependent steps. Training cost grew linearly with sequence length *in
latency*, not just in math.

**2. Long paths.** Information from token 1 reaches token 100 only by
surviving 99 state handoffs. Gradients fought vanishing and exploding
dynamics; LSTMs' gates mitigated but never removed the bottleneck.

**3. One lossy state.** The entire history compresses into a fixed-size
vector. Ask the model to recall an early detail after 200 tokens and you're
asking a summary to be a transcript.

## Attention: content-based lookup

Attention lets every token directly address every other token. Relevance is
computed from *content* — query against key — not from distance in a state
chain (the [mechanics](/roadmap/day/31) are one formula):

$$\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V$$

The three failures dissolve: all positions compute in **parallel** (the
operation is a few big matrix multiplies), the path from token 1 to token 100
is **one hop**, and nothing is compressed — every token attends to the **full
history**.

<Callout kind="deep" title="The real lesson of the Transformer paper">
  The title is the thesis: *Attention Is All You Need*. Removing recurrence
  wasn't a simplification of a good design — it was the removal of the thing
  blocking scale. In ML architecture, the question "what can I delete?" is
  often more valuable than "what can I add?"
</Callout>

## Then scale did the rest

Because training is parallel matrix multiplication, transformers converted
more GPUs directly into better models. The [scaling laws
paper](/papers) showed loss falling predictably with parameters and data —
a curve you could *plan against*. BERT reused the architecture bidirectionally;
GPT made it autoregressive at scale; ViT showed images want it too. One
architecture, every modality — that unification is why it won.

The cost: attention is $O(n^2)$ in sequence length, which is why long context
is an engineering frontier (flash attention, sliding windows, state-space
rivals). The throne is safe for now — but the $n^2$ tax is the reason
researchers keep trying to dethrone it.
