The p-value is the most used and most misunderstood number in data science.
Five specific misconceptions account for almost all of the damage — and each
one dissolves once you've seen a simulation of what the p-value actually is.

## First, the real definition

The p-value is the probability of observing data **at least as extreme as
yours, if the null hypothesis were true**:

$$p = P(\text{data this extreme} \mid H_0)$$

Hold that conditional structure — every misconception below is a violation of
it. [Day 8](/roadmap/day/8) and the [hypothesis testing
lesson](/math/statistics/hypothesis-testing) build this from scratch.

## Misconception 1: "p is the probability H₀ is true"

$$P(H_0 \mid \text{data}) \neq P(\text{data} \mid H_0)$$

The p-value conditions on the hypothesis; belief in the hypothesis conditions
on the data. Flipping the conditional is Bayes' rule territory — the
posterior needs a prior. This confusion is not pedantry: it's the difference
between "this data would be odd under the null" and "the null is probably
false."

## Misconception 2: "p > 0.05 means there's no effect"

Non-significance means *the data didn't distinguish the effect from noise* —
a statement about evidence quantity, not effect existence. Underpowered
studies routinely miss real effects. Report the estimate and its confidence
interval ([Day 19](/roadmap/day/19) shows bootstrap CIs for exactly this
reason).

## Misconception 3: "p = 0.049 and p = 0.051 are different animals"

The 0.05 threshold is a convention for *decision procedures under
repeated sampling*, not a cliff between truth and falsehood. An effect with
p = 0.051 has nearly the same evidence as one with p = 0.049. Treat the
p-value as a continuous measure of compatibility with the null; treat
thresholds as pre-agreed decision rules, and only pre-agreed ones.

## Misconception 4: "small p means large effect"

The p-value entangles effect size with sample size: a tiny effect becomes
"significant" given enough rows. The twin of a significant-but-minuscule
effect is the huge A/B test that ships a change worth nothing. Always pair
the p-value with an effect size and its practical meaning.

## Misconception 5: "we ran 20 tests, one came out at 0.04 — ship it"

Run enough tests and noise will hand you significance: at the 0.05 level, 20
independent null tests expect one "hit." This is the multiple-comparisons
trap — the reason Bonferroni correction and pre-registration exist. [Day
8](/roadmap/day/8) demonstrates it with a simulation you can run yourself.

<Callout kind="warn" title="The garden of forking paths">
  You don't need 20 *planned* tests to hit this trap. Trying three metrics,
  two segments and two time windows is 12 tests. Track everything you looked
  at, not just what won.
</Callout>

## The simulation that makes it stick

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
# Null is TRUE: two identical groups, pure noise
hits = sum(
    stats.ttest_ind(
        rng.normal(0, 1, 100), rng.normal(0, 1, 100)
    ).pvalue < 0.05
    for _ in range(10_000)
)
print(f"{hits/10_000:.1%} of experiments 'found an effect' — with no effect")
```

Roughly 5% of these runs "discover" a significant difference between two
identical distributions. That is the p-value doing exactly what it promises —
and exactly what it doesn't.
