Yesterday you sampled from distributions you chose. Today the spotlight flips:
**the sample mean itself is a random variable**, and its distribution has a
predictable shape no matter where the data came from. That fact is the load-bearing
wall of classical statistics.

## Three levels: population, sample, statistic

- The **population** has some distribution — maybe ugly, maybe unknown.
- A **sample** of size $n$ gives one value of a statistic, say $\bar{x}$.
- Draw the sample again and again: $\bar{x}$ jitters. Its distribution over
  repeated samples is the **sampling distribution** — and it, not the raw
  data, is what error bars describe.

## The CLT and the standard error

$$\bar{X} \approx \mathcal{N}\left(\mu,\ \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right), \qquad SE = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$

Means of large samples are approximately **normal**, centered on the true
mean, with spread that shrinks like $1/\sqrt{n}$: four times the data buys
twice the precision. The population's own shape fades from the picture.

## Simulation: exponential population, Gaussian means

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, experiments = 50, 10_000

means = rng.exponential(scale=2.0, size=(experiments, n)).mean(axis=1)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4))
axes[0].hist(rng.exponential(scale=2.0, size=100_000), bins=100)
axes[0].set_title("Population: exponential, very non-normal")
axes[1].hist(means, bins=100)
axes[1].set_title(f"Sampling distribution of the mean, n={n}")
plt.show()

print("mean of means       :", means.mean())          # ≈ 2.0
print("predicted SE        :", 2.0 / np.sqrt(n))     # 0.283
print("observed std of means:", means.std())          # ≈ 0.283
```

The population is a sharp cliff; the means are a bell. That is the Central
Limit Theorem doing its work in six lines.

## Why A/B tests care

Two group means are each approximately normal, so their **difference** is
approximately normal too — which hands you a known reference distribution to
judge "is this gap too big for chance?" That is the entire engine of the
t-test you will run on Day 8 and the confidence intervals coming later.

<Callout kind="warn" title="n = 30 is a folk rule, not a law">
  The CLT needs a **finite variance** and enough samples to wash out skew.
  Heavy-tailed data (daily returns, session times) may need hundreds of
  observations per mean before the bell appears. Also read the fine print:
  $n$ is the size of *each group*, not the total. When in doubt, rerun
  today's simulation with your own data's shape.
</Callout>
